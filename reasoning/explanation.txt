# Bayesian Inference in SentinelAI — Full Explanation

The Bayesian part of SentinelAI is what turns *"gas is high"* into *"there is an 87% chance this is a fire."* That's the whole point of calling it an AI agent instead of a smoke detector. Let me build it up from the ground.

---

## 1. The Core Idea

Bayes' theorem answers one question:

> **Given what I just observed, how likely is each explanation?**

Written formally:

$$
P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H) \cdot P(H)}{P(E)}
$$

| Symbol      | Name           | Meaning in SentinelAI                           |
|-------------|----------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| **H**       | Hypothesis     | One of: `SAFE`, `GAS_LEAK`, `OVERHEAT`, `FIRE`  |
| **E**       | Evidence       | The sensor features we just extracted           |
| **P(H)**    | **Prior**      | What we believed *before* seeing this reading   |
| **P(E\|H)** | **Likelihood** | How likely this reading is *if H were true*     |
| **P(H\|E)** | **Posterior**  | What we believe *after* seeing the reading      |
| **P(E)**    | Marginal       | Normalizer — makes the four posteriors sum to 1 |

The pipeline in the algorithm is literally this formula applied four times, once per hypothesis.

---

## 2. Why Bayes Fits This Problem

A threshold alarm asks: *"Is gas > 600?"*

Bayes asks: *"Across all sensor evidence, which world am I most likely in?"*

The difference matters because **sensors lie in overlapping ways**:

| Reading      | GAS_LEAK world |OVERHEAT world | FIRE world       |
|--------------|----------------|---------------|------------------|
| Gas high     | ✔ very likely | ✘ unlikely    | ✔ likely         |
| Temp high    | ✘ unlikely    | ✔ very likely | ✔ very likely    |
| Current high | ✘ unlikely    | ✔ likely      | maybe            |
| Flame true   | ✘ rare        | ✘ rare        | ✔ almost certain |

No single sensor is decisive. Bayes **multiplies** the evidence and lets the strongest compatible hypothesis win. That's the whole magic.

---

## 3. The Likelihood Table (the heart of it)

This table encodes our belief about how the world behaves:

```python
LIKELIHOOD = {
    "SAFE":     {"gas_high": 0.05, "air_bad": 0.10, "temp_high": 0.05,
                 "current_high": 0.05, "flame_true": 0.01},
    "GAS_LEAK": {"gas_high": 0.85, "air_bad": 0.80, "temp_high": 0.20,
                 "current_high": 0.10, "flame_true": 0.05},
    "OVERHEAT": {"gas_high": 0.15, "air_bad": 0.30, "temp_high": 0.80,
                 "current_high": 0.75, "flame_true": 0.20},
    "FIRE":     {"gas_high": 0.70, "air_bad": 0.60, "temp_high": 0.90,
                 "current_high": 0.40, "flame_true": 0.95},
}
```

Read one row like a sentence:

> *"If the lab is on fire (H = FIRE), then there is a 90% chance the temperature reads high, a 95% chance the flame sensor fires, and a 70% chance the gas sensor sees something."*

These numbers come from **physical reasoning**, not magic:

- Flame sensors almost never trigger without fire → `P(flame | FIRE) = 0.95`.
- Flame sensors occasionally trigger on sunlight/heat lamps → `P(flame | SAFE) = 0.01`.
- Overheating equipment draws more current → `P(current | OVERHEAT) = 0.75`.
- Gas leaks don't draw extra current → `P(current | GAS_LEAK) = 0.10`.

You can tune these by running controlled tests (put a lighter near the flame sensor, boil water near the DHT22, etc.) and counting how often each condition fires.

---

## 4. The Naïve Bayes Assumption

Real sensors are *correlated* — fire raises temperature **and** triggers flame **and** often raises gas. A full joint probability would require a 2⁵ = 32-entry table per hypothesis.

Naïve Bayes **pretends the sensors are independent given the hypothesis**, so we can just multiply:

$$
P(E \mid H) = P(e_1 \mid H) \cdot P(e_2 \mid H) \cdots P(e_n \mid H)
$$

This is technically wrong (they aren't independent) but it works remarkably well in practice — and it's the standard shortcut in spam filters, medical triage, and embedded inference.

In our code:

```python
p = prior[h]
p *= L["gas_high"]     if f["gas_level"]    > 0.5 else (1 - L["gas_high"])
p *= L["air_bad"]      if f["air_quality"]  > 0.5 else (1 - L["air_bad"])
p *= L["temp_high"]    if f["temp_high"]    > 0.5 else (1 - L["temp_high"])
p *= L["current_high"] if f["current_high"] > 0.5 else (1 - L["current_high"])
p *= L["flame_true"]   if f["flame_true"]              else (1 - L["flame_true"])
```

Each line is a mini Bayes update: *"given this feature, scale my belief."*

The `else (1 - L[...])` part is the **complement** — the probability of *not* seeing that feature given the hypothesis. If gas is high and we're in a SAFE world, we multiply by `1 - 0.05 = 0.95`, which **shrinks** the SAFE belief. That's how negative evidence works.

## 5. Walking Through a Concrete Example

Suppose a fire breaks out. Sensors read:

| Feature     | Value    | Boolean               |
|-------------|----------|-----------------------|
| Gas level   | 0.80     | `gas_high = True`     |
| Air quality | 0.70     | `air_bad = True`      |
| Temp        | 80 °C    | `temp_high = True`    |
| Current     | 1.8 A    | `current_high = True` |
| Flame       | detected | `flame_true = True`   |

Start with uniform priors: `[0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25]`.

### Step 1 — Compute unnormalized posteriors

**SAFE:**
$$
0.25 \times 0.05 \times 0.10 \times 0.05 \times 0.05 \times 0.01 \approx 3.1 \times 10^{-8}
$$

**GAS_LEAK:**
$$
0.25 \times 0.85 \times 0.80 \times 0.20 \times 0.10 \times 0.05 \approx 1.7 \times 10^{-4}
$$

**OVERHEAT:**
$$
0.25 \times 0.15 \times 0.30 \times 0.80 \times 0.75 \times 0.20 \approx 1.35 \times 10^{-3}
$$

**FIRE:**
$$
0.25 \times 0.70 \times 0.60 \times 0.90 \times 0.40 \times 0.95 \approx 3.59 \times 10^{-2}
$$

### Step 2 — Normalize

Sum ≈ `0.0374`. Divide each by the sum:

| Hypothesis | Raw         | Posterior |
|------------|-------------|-----------|
| SAFE       | 3.1 × 10⁻⁸  | **0.000** |
| GAS_LEAK   | 1.7 × 10⁻⁴  | **0.005** |
| OVERHEAT   | 1.35 × 10⁻³ | **0.036** |
| FIRE       | 3.59 × 10⁻² | **0.959** |

The agent now believes **95.9% FIRE**. That's the confidence reported to the dashboard, and it drives a RED alert.

Notice: even *though* the gas sensor alone could have suggested GAS_LEAK, the flame + temp + current combination pushed FIRE far ahead. That's the fusion.

## 6. Temporal Bayesian Update (why it's recursive)

The algorithm does something subtle:

```python
self.beliefs = bayesian_update(f, self.beliefs)   # prior = last posterior
```

The **posterior of cycle N becomes the prior of cycle N+1**. This is the Bayesian filter (same math as a Kalman filter / HMM forward pass). It means:

- A single noisy reading can't flip the belief (evidence multiplies).
- Sustained evidence rapidly drives confidence toward certainty.
- Sudden discontinuities are smoothed by prior inertia.

Combined with the confirmation counter (require N cycles before switching state), this kills the flapping you'd see from raw thresholds.

---

## 7. Rule Evidence as a Multiplier

Pure Bayes can be *too* smooth. If a flame sensor triggers and the temp hits 90 °C, you want an immediate, aggressive response — not a gradual belief shift.

That's why after the Bayesian update:

```python
for h, e in evidence.items():
    if h in self.beliefs and e > 0:
        self.beliefs[h] *= (1 + 0.15 * e)
```

Rule evidence multiplies the belief. A rule firing with strength 5 boosts that hypothesis by `1 + 0.15×5 = 1.75×`. This is a hybrid:

- **Bayes** = smooth, probabilistic baseline.
- **Rules** = hard, fast overrides for known emergencies.

Re-normalizing after keeps the beliefs a valid probability distribution.

## 8. Common Pitfalls (worth knowing for your viva)

| Pitfall             | What happens                                            | Fix                                          |
|---------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Zero likelihoods    | One `P(E\|H)=0` zeroes the whole product                | Use 0.01 instead of 0.00 (Laplace smoothing) |
| Wrong priors        | Starting with `[0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1]` biases toward SAFE | Start uniform, or learn from data            | 
| Correlated sensors  | Naïve Bayes over-counts redundant evidence              | Group features or use a Bayesian network     |
| Numerical underflow | Products of many small numbers → 0 on ESP32 floats      | Work in **log space**: `log P = log prior + Σ log likelihood` |
| Stale likelihoods   | Real sensors drift                                      | Recalibrate `LIKELIHOOD` per lab             |

For ESP32 specifically, **log-space** is worth adopting — five features multiplied by four hypotheses with small floats can underflow `float32` after a few cycles. In log space you just add:

```python
import math
log_post[h] = log(prior[h]) + sum(log(L[k] if cond else 1 - L[k]) ...)
```

Then `posterior[h] = exp(log_post[h] - max(log_post))` for numerical stability, and normalize.

## 9. Why This Counts as "AI"

For your syllabus write-up, Bayesian inference is typically classified under:

- **Probabilistic reasoning** — reasoning with uncertainty
- **Knowledge representation** — the likelihood table encodes domain knowledge
- **Inference under uncertainty** — the update equation
- **Sensor fusion** — combining multiple noisy sources

It's one of the oldest and most respectable AI techniques (going back to the 1760s), and it's *the* correct tool for this problem. Your ESP32 is running the same math a Mars rover uses to weigh fault hypotheses — just with cheaper sensors.

---

## 10. One-Paragraph Summary

SentinelAI keeps a belief distribution over four states — SAFE, GAS_LEAK, OVERHEAT, FIRE. Each cycle, it treats the previous beliefs as priors and multiplies in the likelihood of each sensor's reading under each hypothesis. It normalizes to get a probability distribution, boosts hypotheses whose rules fired, then picks the maximum. The result is a smooth, principled, uncertainty-aware answer to *"what is actually happening in this lab?"* — which is exactly what makes the difference between a smoke alarm and an agent.
